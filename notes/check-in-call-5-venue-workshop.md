## TL;DR

- A **venue is not a sponsor**: don't ask for money and a venue in one email. Venues are letting **random teenage strangers** into their building, so **trust** is the whole game — say who you are, be specific, be short, offer a call.
- **Build a list of ~25 venues (the deck) — the speaker recommends 30–40** — across three tiers (innovators / normal good offices / literally anywhere), find **real people** (not `info@`) via Google + LinkedIn, and send **personalised** emails: "**50 good emails beat 200 bad ones**".
- **Follow up every other day.** Sending was only half the job. **Call** (Google Maps phone numbers; ask reception who the right person is).
- If you're still without a venue: aim for a **12-hour** (or 8+8/10+10-hour split) booking, not 24 h. **No AI-written emails/images**, **never** sell attendee data, don't use a Hack Club HCB card for LinkedIn Premium.
- Everyone who stayed has a **venue extension to Sun 18 Oct**.

### [20:30–22:30] Day 1 · the prospectus — "a venue is not a sponsor" *(slides 4–6)*
- Slide: *"who here is stuck finding a venue? … good news: there is a boring, repeatable way to fix it. We are about to speed-run it."*
- **A venue is not a sponsor**: sponsors give money, venues give their space. "You are asking them to let a bunch of random strangers walk into their building (who are also teenagers)", so "they need way more convincing — yes, we look suspicious on paper."
- Don't ask for money and a venue in the same email: use the **foot-in-the-door** idea (ask for something small first). Prove you're real, that you won't steal anything or break anything, and that you want to **make a good impact** in your city.

### [22:30–24:00] What goes inside a prospectus *(slide 8)*
- **The stuff:** who we are; **every detail of the event**; the **schedule/timeline**; what we're looking for; what they can expect from us.
- **Be specific:** explain what a hackathon is if they don't know ("a coding event, people eat food and make cool projects"); have a **date and time** in mind, e.g. *"a venue for 12 hours on 14 November, 9 am–9 pm"* rather than "can we have a venue?".
- **Share photos:** real photos of past events — **local first**, otherwise any Hack Club photos (the craziest ones), or even a non-Hack-Club hackathon as long as people are visibly coding and happy. **No AI images, ever** ("AI art in a trust document? that's bold"); **don't write AI-generated emails either.**
- **Photo sources (staff add):** a **drive of Campfire photos**, a **GitHub repo of photos from previous hackathons**, and **`photos.hackclub.com`**.

### [24:00–25:00] Need a prospectus? Steal ours *(slide 9)*
- HQ built a **Haven prospectus template**: make a copy and swap in your city and details. The deck author's slides have a link to it; a staff-posted **Canva link is in the chat** (see Links). The speaker also points to an example email in the **organizer guide** ("[unclear] email") for inspiration. Kaylee shares the deck link afterwards (via #haven-organizers-bulletin).

### [25:00–26:30] Trust, step 1: say who you are *(slides 11, 14)*
- Say who you are and what you're doing, in the email itself. Example opener: *"Hi, my name is Kaylee and I'm an 18-year-old trying to organise a coding event in my city."* — quick, to the point, personable.
- **If you're international**: explain **Hack Club is a nonprofit based in the US**; the name sounds off-putting, so say you **help teenagers make awesome projects** (not illegal things).
- **If they ask for proof:** the official **fiscal sponsorship documents** are in your **HCB account, inside your HCB organisation** (IRS forms, etc.) — send them when asked.
- **Event insurance** is **not** covered by Hack Club; if you have funds (sponsor money or leftover signup funds) you can buy it; a staff member says the insurance helper is **US-only** — reach out to staff for getting insurance.

### [26:30–29:00] What goes where; keep it short *(slides 15–19)*
- **In the email:** a tiny Hack Club intro, who you are, **the ask** (e.g. "this space, on this day, from this time"), a **short call offer**. **In the prospectus:** photos of past events, the **event schedule**, the **full proposal**. "Email short, prospectus = the full proposal."
- Mention the venue requirements from the **Organizer Guide: Wi-Fi, an AV system, and outlets** — a venue without Wi-Fi means renting a router (more cost).
- **The bigger the email, the more it sounds like a cold call** — write like "an excited teenager", scannable (people read email on the go). "Cold calls are for people who sell warranties."
- **Tell them what they are *not* being asked for** (slide 17): **logistics, food, everything is managed by the local organisers (you); the budget is backed by Hack Club; the venue only gives the space.** ("Easiest yes of their week.") Mentioning that **you handle catering** and that you'll have **adult chaperones (which are required)** also reassures venues who worry about staff/security.
- **End with a call offer** (slide 19): "happy to explain everything on a call" — 15 or 25 minutes; a voice builds trust faster. Keeps you from being "just a cold email".

### [29:00–31:30] Sell Hack Club *(slides 21–24)*
- "What Hack Club is (make us sound cool)": **a charity that helps teenagers get into technical things**; run amazing programs — flex a little.
- **Programs that sound very cool** (slide 23): **Stardance** (a Hack Club challenge with **NASA and AMD**, still running; both have official articles you can link), **Summer of Making**, **hackathons Hack Club has run in your area** (list only real ones), past events like **Daydream and Campfire**; a recap **YouTube video** of the previous game-jam event is worth linking. Well-known donors/backers include **Tom Preston-Werner (GitHub co-founder)** — see `hackclub.com`.
- **Everything is open source, even the money** (slide 24): Hack Club is open source and has its own neobank for teens, **HCB**, whose finances are **public** — a venue or sponsor can see you're spending on food, "not on a new iPad". Tech-company venues like the open-source angle.

### [31:30–33:00] Perks, shout-outs and safeguarding *(slides 26–27)*
- **Give them a shout-out:** recognise the venue as a **"venue sponsor"**, put their **logo/link on your website**, free credit for them.
- **Pitch a win-win** (slide 27): a company can **reserve seats for employees' kids**; a university can get **seats for its tech club** — "they get to do something nice for their people; you get free signups".
- **Safeguarding warning:** you are **not obliged to give venues or sponsors your attendees' emails/data** (e.g. a tech company wanting to recruit). **These are children**; data-privacy laws apply, and you agreed to terms when setting up your HCB organisation that limit this. "Please don't sell your attendees' data to venues or sponsors."

### [33:00–36:00] Day 2 · the venue list — three tiers *(slides 29–33)*
- **Tier 1: the innovators** (slide 29) — venues doing something new in your area (a new-age college, a new office of a YC founder; e.g. the Founders School venue in New York). Rural areas may have few; urban areas/Bay Area plenty. "Generous and curious people". Startups are often keener since it gets their name out.
- **Tier 2: normal good offices** (slide 30) — tech offices, universities, tech-based non-profits; also any corporate office/**co-working space** already equipped with AV, outlets, etc. **University CS/STEM clubs** may already host hackathons — partner with the club (an example given: **Haven Charlotte** partnered with a Girls Who Code club at their local university).
- **Tier 3: literally anywhere** (slide 31) — **cafés** (if they'll lend space for 12 h), **public libraries**, **schools** (your STEM club advisor can chaperone — "yes, you can ask your own school"), **museums, non-profits, YMCA, Boy Scouts, Boys & Girls Clubs**. "Free is free."
- **How many:** the deck suggests a list of **~25 venues** — about **10 tier-1**, then tier-2 (universities/offices), then tier-3 (schools/libraries); the presenter would **go for 30–40 in total**.
- **How to find them** (slide 33): Google **"top 10 companies in [your city]"**, **"top startup founders in [your city]"**; **Google Maps** (look around you; places show address, website, sometimes phone); tier-1 hint: the **newest startups and fresh offices**.

### [36:00–37:30] A warning about AI *(slide 34)*
- Deck: **don't ask AI to "find me something new in my area"** — it gives a generic reply. Use **Google Search for places**, AI for news if you like, and **never to write the email**.
- Presenter's nuance: an AI tool can be a **starting point for a list of ~30 candidate venues**, but **verify each** (sites can be out of date or inactive).

### [37:30–38:15] Do NOT bulk-email *(slide 35)*
- Emailing ~200 venues gets **way fewer replies**; email **~25 different venues manually**, **researching each one**. "50 good emails beat 200 bad ones." Add **one personal sentence** per venue (e.g. *"we love that your library runs game-day workshops…"*).
- **Split venues across co-organisers** — emailing and following up 40 venues is too much for one person; make each person responsible for a handful. (Slide: "Split the work with other organisers".)

### [38:15–40:00] Finding real humans: company + position *(slides 36–43)*
- **Don't use the general `info@` / business address or a contact-us form** (deck: use a real, named person instead — a general inbox or form is unlikely to reach someone who can say yes).
- **Step 2: company name + position.** Worked example with **Microsoft**: Google *"Microsoft marketing head"*; for big companies target **marketing, community managers, developer relations** ("it looks good on them to support teen-hosted game jams"); for a **local startup**: **VP, CEO, founder, marketing, director** ("CEOs are humans as well"). Google results are local; if not, add your city.
- **Step 3: find the LinkedIn profiles** (slide 43) of relevant people; repeat for every position that might matter — "you can get a lot of contacts from just one venue". A real example (per the speaker): one of the Gap Year people messaged someone at **Unity** on LinkedIn and was touring their office in Canada that day.

### [40:30–42:45] Universities and schools *(slides 44–45)*
- **University:** **student relations manager**, a **professor** (CS/engineering) or **managing director**; **school:** the **principal** (teachers may need principal permission, so going straight to the principal is often easier) — but still talk to teachers, who can be your adult chaperone. A **career counsellor** (US: counsellor) can **email-blast your school's students**.
- Look for **board pages** and **faculty profiles/staff directories** (example from Delhi); for a **startup** check its **team page**. If a name has no email, look the name up on LinkedIn.

### [42:45–44:45] LinkedIn connection requests *(slides 46–48)*
- **Step 4:** open the "…" menu → **Connect**. "Be brave for 4 seconds."
- **Add a note (careful):** free LinkedIn lets you personalise only **about 5 requests/month** — save them for people likely to reply. Sample: *"Hey, I am a teenager and I am organising this, I wanted to talk about this."* **Premium** gives more notes; you can get **one month free as a trial**. **Remember to cancel it, and do NOT use your HCB card** for LinkedIn Premium (it's a subscription not essential to your event — could be treated as HCB misuse; your account may be banned).
- **Send it even if your profile is meh** — no LinkedIn? make one today. "A lot of them won't accept, that is fine. We have another way." Networking pays off: someone who made a LinkedIn post about their Campfire landed a job with a company (**$30/hour** per the speaker; chat said "40 an hour") and still works there. A similar **Cloudflare** story was mentioned for the Haven Portugal/Lisbon POC (garbled in the audio).

### [45:00–46:30] They didn't accept? Google their email *(slides 49–50)*
- Search *"[name] [company] email"*: you'll find articles or sites like **RocketReach** with the address indexed — **email only, never phone**. Found several? **Email each address.** Free lookups run out fast: **split them across your team, each on their own account** (or make accounts with temp mail).
- Staff add that **Apollo.io** worked well for them for a past event (and chat users like it); a Slack **#temp-email** channel exists for temp addresses.

### [46:30–48:10] Google Maps: call reception *(slides 52–53)*
- "We are not done": type the venue name into **Google Maps**, find the **reception phone number**, **call, say you are organising an event, and ask who the right person is**, then **email that person**. A call tells you in **5 seconds** whether they're interested.
- **What reception might say** (slide 53): they give you a **number**, a **time to call**, or an **email** — "any of these is a win" (not a rejection, and your name is now out there). Replies are either **forwarded to a better person** (one step closer) or **it's the right person** and they may say yes.

### [48:10–50:15] The next two days: reach out *(slides 55–57)*
- **Don't send cold emails** — make one template but **personalise every outreach**; spread it over **two days**; **split the work** with co-organisers; **book a call with an EM/director to review your email**.
- **Don't contact a venue only one way:** reach **up to 5 different people** at the same place (VP, director, CEO/founder, marketing head) and split these across your team. A staff anecdote: **Haven Denver** got in touch with **the CTO of Palantir** (through a connection) and **got a $1,000 sponsorship** — but **do not cold-email a CTO, and you cannot take military sponsorships for Haven events (no drone companies)**.
- **Still no venue?** **Don't go for a 24-hour (or two-day) venue** — it's probably hard; **keep to 12-hour (or "12-12") bookings**, or **split 8+8 / 10+10-hour** — "a good short time frame improves your chances".

### [50:15–51:15] The other half: the next 5 days *(slide 59)*
- You've now reached out to all the venues. **Next 5 days: wait for replies, take calls, and follow up with all of them — sending emails was only half the job.** Follow up **every other day** — "if they really want you to stop, they can email you back; at least you got a response" (chat: "my follow-up email turned into a Zoom meeting with the venue").

### [51:15–52:15] Extension and signups
- **Announcement (staff):** "If you're still here, you have a **venue extension from the 10th to the 18th** — you now have until **18 Oct** to get a venue."
- **Signups don't depend on a venue** — "we need signups" (to size shirts and so you have attendees). **Prioritise the venue, but keep getting signups.**
- **Money for a venue:** hopefully the venue is free, but if one asks for money and **your budget has room** (from signup funds or sponsorships, per the budget template), **you can use that money to book it**. Signups can effectively get you a venue. *(In chat, one POC reported a venue asking for a 50 % deposit and a certificate of insurance; another reported 25 %; and an Australian POC mentioned a venue asking $4,000. No official answer to these was given in the chat export.)*

### [52:15–53:55] One last tip: look human *(slide 60)*
- An email with a **name + a real photo** is more likely to be opened; **use your face, not the Haven logo** ("they don't know Haven yet").
- **Personal vs. Hack Club email?** Presenters' view: **personal email gives a personal touch**, the **Hack Club one proves you're official**; recommendation: **use the one with your face and CC your Hack Club email**, and **redirect to the Hack Club email once you're in active talks**. If using a personal address, make it **professional** (not "ihateeveryone123"). "Make it seem like you're human, because you are."

### [53:55–57:20] Thank-you, a Code Ninjas tip, wrap-up *(slide 61)*
- Slide: *"questions? ask me anything. I reply faster than a venue does. Or book a call with an EM before you do with the venue."*
- **Last-minute tip from staff:** check for a **Code Ninjas** in your city — their **franchisee contract has a clause to help fund and sponsor community events**; they "love running hackathons/game jams" (a staffer who also worked at Code Ninjas says they were given the keys overnight for their Campfire) — "they would love to host you."
